## Explainer

### What the ship is

Starship's upper stage, usually called "the ship", is a stainless-steel spacecraft about 52 m tall and 9 m wide that does three jobs a traditional rocket splits between separate pieces: it is the second stage that finishes the climb to orbit, it is the payload fairing that carries cargo, and it is a reentry vehicle designed to come home and be reused. SpaceX lists the current (V3) ship at 52 m, 1,600 t of propellant, 1,614 tf of total vacuum thrust, and more than 100 t of payload to orbit in a fully reusable configuration (SpaceX vehicle page; SpaceX Form S-1).

From the top down: a nosecone holding two small "header" tanks and many high-pressure gas bottles, a short payload bay, a methane tank, a larger liquid-oxygen tank, and an unpressurized engine bay (the "skirt") with six Raptor engines. Four flaps steer it through the atmosphere, and black hexagonal tiles cover the windward half.

### How the propellant is arranged

The two main tanks share a single steel wall called the common dome. Methane (fuel) sits above it and liquid oxygen sits below it, so the tank walls are the outer skin of the ship with no separate insulation. The common dome bulges downward so that methane drains to its centre, where a large pipe (the fuel transfer tube or downcomer) runs down through the LOX tank to the engines (Ringwatchers s33-tanks).

For V2, SpaceX added about 300 t of propellant (a 25 percent increase) by stretching the ship one 1.8 m ring and by flattening the domes so the tanks could reach into space that the payload bay was not using (Ringwatchers, quoting SpaceX). V3 keeps the same outside height but moves the common dome and the aft dome down by about 1.1 m and slightly recesses the vacuum engines into the bottom of the LOX tank, which adds roughly another 100 t (NSF, 2026-03-03 and 2026-05-15). SpaceX does not publish the LOX and methane split; at an oxidizer-to-fuel ratio of about 3.6, 1,600 t works out to roughly 1,250 t of LOX and 350 t of methane (estimate).

### Why there are header tanks

When the ship falls belly-first through the atmosphere, the leftover propellant in the big tanks sloshes against the side walls. The engines need liquid, not a froth of gas, at their inlets when they relight for landing. So the ship carries two small reserve tanks, one for LOX and one for methane, just for the landing flip and burn. On the orbital-class ships (from Ship 24 onward, and on V2 and V3) both headers live in the nosecone: the LOX header forms the very tip of the nose, and the methane header is a sphere hanging directly beneath it (Ringwatchers s33-nose; NSF 2026-03-13). V3's LOX header part was labelled "V3 LOX CONE" when first spotted (NSF), and SpaceX says V3 has "100% vacuum jacketing coverage of the header feed system", meaning the long pipes from the nose down to the engines are insulated like a thermos so the propellant stays cold on long coasts (SpaceX, 2026-05-12).

### Engines and steering

The ship has six Raptor engines: three sea-level Raptors in the centre and three Raptor Vacuum engines (RVacs) around them. On V3 all six are Raptor 3, rated at 250 tf (sea level) and 275 tf (vacuum) (SpaceX, 2026-05-12). The RVac's much larger nozzle extracts more energy in vacuum but cannot run safely at sea level, which is why only the three sea-level engines are used for landing.

Only the three sea-level engines gimbal (tilt on actuators to steer); the RVacs are fixed (Ringwatchers s33-nose and s33-aft refer to "the three gimballing engines"). During ascent the ship steers with the gimballing engines and, if an engine is lost, burns the others longer. That exact case happened on Flights 12 and 14, when one RVac shut down early and the ship still reached its planned trajectory (SpaceX Flight 12 and 14 pages). For in-space burns (orbit insertion and deorbit on Flight 14), SpaceX used a single sea-level Raptor (SpaceX Flight 14 page).

### The flaps

Starship has no wings. Four flaps act like a skydiver's arms and legs. Folding a flap in reduces drag at that end, and swinging it out increases it, so moving the fore and aft pairs differentially pitches, rolls and yaws the falling ship. On V1 the forward flaps sat 180 degrees apart like the aft pair. Musk said in 2021 that this was "a slight error", because the fixed part of each forward flap pushed the nose backwards. V2 moved the forward flaps higher, made them thinner and swung them toward the leeward side, about 140 degrees apart, so they are mostly hidden from the hot flow when stowed (Ringwatchers s33-nose). V3 keeps the V2 forward flaps and the same size aft flaps. It changes the aft flap drive from two actuators per flap to one actuator with three motors, for redundancy on return-to-launch-site landings (SpaceX, 2026-05-12). The flaps are driven by electric motors; early prototypes used Tesla car motors and battery packs (Teslarati, 2021-10-04), but the current motor supplier and battery design are not public.

### The heat shield

The windward side is covered in hexagonal ceramic tiles, about 18,000 of them on the early orbital ships (CNBC, 2021, quoting Musk). They are reported to be silica-based. Hexagons interlock so that, in Musk's words, there is "no straight path for hot gas to accelerate through the gaps" (Engadget, 2019). A 2019 torch test heated samples to about 1,650 K, which Musk called orbital entry temperature. Each tile clips onto small pins welded to the steel skin rather than being glued, because glued tiles were the ones that most often fell off in early flights. Under the tiles is a white felt insulation blanket, and under that, since Ship 30 (Flight 5), a black ablative backup layer that chars away to protect the steel if a tile is lost (Ringwatchers s33-tps and s37-tps). Felt "gap filler" was later packed between tiles after Flight 10 showed hot gas seeping through gaps.

V3 introduced a simpler tile pin, removed a horizontal tile seam on the nose, and pinned nearly every tile, with glue left only at the nose tip and a few spots (NSF, 2026-02-27 and 2026-05-15). Ship 41 (Flight 14) added an extended ablative layer, extra retention on tiles that had loosened, and a curved-tile experiment (NSF, 2026-09-27). SpaceX also tried metallic tiles on Flight 10 (three on Ship 37, one actively cooled); Bill Gerstenmaier later said they "didn't work so well" (as reported from his September 2025 talk). Some strips of tiles along the sides have been left off on purpose so the tower's chopstick arms can slide along the hull during a catch without scraping tiles off (Ringwatchers s37-tps, quoting SpaceX's Dan Huot).

### The payload bay

Today's ship is optimised for Starlink. Instead of a giant clamshell door, it has a slot-shaped door and a "PEZ dispenser" that pushes flat satellites out one at a time, like candy from the toy. On V2 the payload barrel shrank to three rings (about 5.5 m), but stronger nosecone framing made the lower part of the nose usable, so usable volume changed little (Ringwatchers s33-pez). V3 upgrades the dispenser with new actuators and inverters for faster release (SpaceX, 2026-05-12) and adds vents to depressurise the bay before the door opens (NSF, 2026-05-15). Flight 14 deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites into orbit, the first operational payload delivered by Starship (SpaceX Flight 14 page).

### Getting home: reentry, flip and landing

After its mission the ship performs a deorbit burn and falls into the atmosphere belly-first at orbital speed (about 17,500 mph in low Earth orbit), with the tiled side facing the flow. The flaps hold it at a high angle of attack so the broad belly acts as a brake, much like a skydiver lying flat. SpaceX has not published the exact angles or the peak heat load. Recent flights added a "dynamic banking maneuver" near the end to mimic the path of a return to Starbase (SpaceX Flight 11 to 13 pages).

A few hundred metres above the surface the ship relights its sea-level engines, fed by the header tanks, and swings from horizontal to vertical in a few seconds: the "landing flip". It then slows with a landing burn. On Flight 12 the plan was three engines, then two, then one, and SpaceX says the ship splashed down on two engines. On Flights 13 and 14 all three sea-level Raptors relit for the flip and burn (SpaceX mission pages; NSF, 2026-05-20). Ship 40 (Flight 13) was the first ship to survive splashdown intact. It was towed to Christmas Island after about 24 days at sea, and SpaceX collected heat-shield samples from it (Space.com, 2026-08-20).

The goal is to catch the ship with the launch tower's chopsticks, as SpaceX already does with the booster. V3 replaced the old lift sockets with new catch/lift points higher on the nose (NSF, 2026-05-15). Ship 39 was squeeze-tested on the ground to simulate catch loads, and in September 2026 Ship 42 was lifted by the Pad 2 chopsticks and reconnected to the propellant arm as a catch rehearsal (NSF, 2026-09-14). On 2026-08-20 Musk said SpaceX "will probably catch the ship with the tower in a few months" (Space.com). Flight 15 is the next candidate, pending FAA approval (NSF, 2026-09-25).

### Docking and ship-to-ship refueling

Missions to the Moon and Mars need a ship refilled in low Earth orbit. V3 adds four docking drogues on the leeward side, two near the payload bay and two at the aft end, which are the passive half of a probe-and-drogue docking system (SpaceX, 2026-05-12; NSF, 2026-05-15). SpaceX says V3 ships "can be configured to act as tanker vehicles with the addition of docking probes", and that the ground-fill connection has been updated to double as the in-space transfer port (SpaceX, "To the Moon and Beyond", 2025-10-30). On V3 that ship quick-disconnect plate is split into separate LOX and methane halves, sitting between the two aft drogues (NSF). Rendezvous will use DragonEye sensors from Dragon, and radio-frequency gauges will measure propellant in microgravity. The only transfer demonstrated so far is about 5 t of LOX moved from a header tank to a main tank inside one ship on Flight 3 (2024). As of 2026-09-29, no ship-to-ship transfer has been attempted; SpaceX says orbital flights will enable "the first orbital propellant transfer" (SpaceX, 2026-09-15).

### Power, communications and control

V3 uses about 60 custom avionics units across ship and booster, each combining batteries, inverters and high-voltage distribution, with about 9 MW of peak power across both vehicles. Starlink provides 480 Mbps of redundant links and about 50 camera views (SpaceX, 2026-05-12). In space, small thrusters control attitude. Historically these were cold-gas thrusters fed by boil-off ("ullage") gas from the tanks. SpaceX calls V3's system redesigned and "more efficient", with new roll thrusters on the payload bay, a mid-body set, and thrusters fed through the aft flap hinges (NSF, 2026-05-15); their propellant and thrust are not published.

### Variants

SpaceX has described several configurations of the same basic ship:

- **Starlink ship:** PEZ door; flying now.
- **Tanker:** a V3 ship fitted with docking probes.
- **Propellant depot:** a long-duration storage ship; SpaceX has tested a depot power module.
- **Human Landing System (HLS):** a crewed lunar lander with no heat shield or flaps. It has landing legs, an elevator, two airlocks and more than 600 m3 of pressurized volume, and docks to Orion with an androgynous docking system derived from Dragon's.
- **Cargo lander:** delivers up to 100 t to the lunar surface.
- **Crew ship:** SpaceX says up to 100 people on long-duration flights.

SpaceX Form S-1 says future ships could lift 200 t, "potentially as soon as Starship V4". Musk's May 2025 presentation showed a ship with nine engines (six RVacs) for that version (NSF, 2025-05-30).

1. **id: `lox-header-tank`**

   - **Function:** Holds reserve liquid oxygen for the landing flip and burn.
   - **Position:** 0.95 to 1.00 (estimate).
   - **Detail:** The tank's upper wall is the nose tip itself, and a conical sump at the bottom leads to a vacuum-jacketed transfer line running the length of the ship.    - **Specs:** Capacity not public.
   - **Sources:** Ringwatchers s33-nose; NSF 2026-05-15; SpaceX 2026-05-12.

2. **id: `nose-vents-rcs`**
   - **Name:** Nose vents and pitch thruster
   - **Function:** Vent header-tank boil-off and provide attitude control near the nose.
   - **Position:** 0.92 to 0.97 (estimate).
   - **Detail:** On V2, bidirectional vents on each side of the nose (two per header tank) are staggered so oxygen and methane plumes do not mix. A single tile-covered deflected vent likely acts as a pitch thruster. NSF reported "new vents beneath the nose-cone tiles" on V3.
   - **Sources:** Ringwatchers s33-nose; NSF 2026-03-03.

3. **id: `ch4-header-tank`**
   - **Name:** Methane header tank
   - **Function:** Holds reserve liquid methane for landing.
   - **Position:** 0.89 to 0.95 (estimate).
   - **Detail:** A sphere hanging directly beneath the LOX header inside the nosecone. On V2 it hangs from a conical support structure welded to the nose wall; before that it hung from six pairs of struts. The claim that the methane header sits "in the LOX tank" does not match the orbital-class ships (Ship 24 onward) or V2/V3.
   - **Specs:** Capacity not public.
   - **Sources:** Ringwatchers s33-nose; NSF 2026-03-13.

4. **id: `nose-copvs`**
   - **Name:** Composite overwrapped pressure vessels (COPVs)
   - **Function:** Store high-pressure gases for engine igniters, turbopump spin-up, purges, pneumatic valves and header pressurization.
   - **Position:** 0.85 to 0.93 (estimate), clustered around the methane header, mostly on the windward side. More sit at the base of the payload bay, around 0.63 to 0.66.
   - **Detail:** NSF counted at least 32 COPVs on Ship 39, more than on V1 or V2, because Raptor 3 needs more gases held in orbit for longer. After Ship 36 was destroyed on a test stand in June 2025 by a damaged payload-bay COPV (nitrogen for environmental control), SpaceX lowered COPV operating pressures and added inspections and protective covers.
   - **Sources:** NSF 2026-05-15; SpaceX "Flight 9 and Ship 36 Report" 2025-08-15; Ringwatchers s33-nose.

5. **id: `forward-flaps`**
   - **Name:** Forward flaps (2)
   - **Function:** Pitch and roll control at the nose during atmospheric descent.
   - **Position:** 0.77 to 0.90 (estimate).
   - **Detail:** Since V2 they sit higher on the nose and shifted toward the leeward side, about 140 degrees apart, and are thinner, constant-thickness and swept. Each attaches at three hinge points, with a separate electrically driven control arm (one arm per flap on V2, per Ringwatchers inference). Aerocovers seal the hinges. V3's forward flaps are externally similar to V2's.
   - **Specs:** Dimensions not public.
   - **Sources:** Ringwatchers s33-nose; NSF 2026-05-15; SpaceX 2026-05-12.

6. **id: `catch-points`**
   - **Name:** Catch and lift points
   - **Function:** Hard points that rest on the tower chopsticks for lifting and, in future, catching the ship.
   - **Position:** about 0.76 to 0.80 (estimate), one on each side.
   - **Detail:** V3 deleted the earlier lift sockets used with the chopsticks and added newly designed catch/lift points higher up on the nose. V2 had first non-structural, then (from Ship 35) structural catch fittings, plus a tile-free strip where the arms slide. Ship 39 was squeeze-tested to mimic catch forces, and Ship 42 completed chopsticks lift and hold tests in September 2026.
   - **Sources:** NSF 2026-05-15, 2026-03-03, 2026-09-14; Ringwatchers s37-tps.

   - **Position:** Forward section, about 0.74 to 0.80 (uncertain; see Uncertain).
   - **Detail:** V3 rearranged the four dishes: two on the sides of the heat shield and two on the leeward side, for better coverage in orbit. They provide 480 Mbps of redundant connectivity.
   - **Sources:** NSF 2026-05-15 and 2026-03-03; SpaceX 2026-05-12.

8. **id: `nosecone-structure`**
   - **Name:** Nosecone
   - **Function:** Aerodynamic and structural cap; houses the header tanks, COPVs and flap mounts, and forms part of the payload volume.
   - **Position:** 0.74 to 1.00 (estimate).

9. **id: `payload-bay`**
   - **Name:** Payload bay
   - **Function:** Carries cargo; currently configured for Starlink.
   - **Position:** About 0.63 (top of the forward dome) to 0.80 (usable space extends into the lower nosecone) (estimate).
   - **Detail:** The payload barrel is three rings, about 5.5 m. V3 adds vents to equalize bay pressure before the door opens. SpaceX's S-1 says the bay volume is "rivaling the pressurized sections of the International Space Station", a statement aimed at future configurations.

10. **id: `pez-dispenser`**
    - **Name:** PEZ dispenser and payload door
    - **Function:** Deploys flat Starlink satellites one at a time through a slot door.
    - **Position:** Door about 0.66 to 0.71 (estimate); the dispenser stack sits on the forward dome.
    - **Detail:** The dispenser is mounted to the forward dome, and a track pushes each satellite out, then the next one is lowered into place. The small door lets the bay stay partly pressure-stabilized, avoiding a huge hinged door. V2 made the door fully rounded. V3 adds new actuators and inverters for faster deployment. It deployed 20 simulators plus 2 camera satellites (Flight 12), 20 Starlink V3 (Flight 13) and 26 Starlink V3 into orbit (Flight 14).
    - **Sources:** SpaceX 2026-05-12 and mission pages; Ringwatchers s33-pez.

11. **id: `docking-drogues-forward`**
    - **Name:** Forward docking drogues (2)
    - **Function:** Passive half of the probe-and-drogue system for ship-to-ship docking.
    - **Position:** about 0.64 to 0.72, leeward side (estimate).
    - **Detail:** SpaceX lists four drogues on the leeward side in total. A tanker gets active probes. The system has not yet been used in flight.
    - **Sources:** SpaceX 2026-05-12, 2025-10-30; NSF 2026-05-15.

12. **id: `rcs-payload-bay`**
    - **Name:** Payload-bay roll thrusters
    - **Function:** Roll and fine attitude control, including for docking.
    - **Position:** about 0.66 to 0.73 (estimate).
    - **Detail:** Two pairs facing opposite directions, new on V3 as part of a redesigned reaction control system. Additional roll thrusters were first added after Flight 3 lost roll control from clogged valves.
    - **Sources:** NSF 2026-05-15; SpaceX "On the Path to Rapid Reusability" 2024-05-24.

13. **id: `forward-dome`**
    - **Name:** Forward dome
    - **Function:** Top bulkhead of the methane tank and floor of the payload bay.
    - **Position:** weld about 0.60, crown about 0.63 (estimate).
    - **Detail:** Since V2 it is a flatter "e-dome" of 18 stretch-formed panels, replacing 36 stamped panels. The main-tank pressurization diffuser sits on it; its failure caused the Flight 9 loss and it was redesigned. V3 moves the flight termination system charges into the raceway at this dome.
    - **Sources:** Ringwatchers s33-tanks; SpaceX Flight 9 report; NSF 2026-05-15.

14. **id: `ch4-main-tank`**
    - **Name:** Methane (fuel) main tank
    - **Function:** Holds most of the liquid methane.
    - **Position:** about 0.42 (common-dome weld) to 0.60 (estimate); the common dome crown dips to about 0.38.
    - **Detail:** Stainless-steel wall doubling as the vehicle skin. On V2 it has about 30 columns of internal stringers and three slosh baffles. V3 enlarged it by moving the common dome down about 1.1 m.
    - **Specs:** About 350 t methane (estimate).
    - **Sources:** Ringwatchers s33-tanks; NSF 2026-05-15.

15. **id: `raceway`**
    - **Name:** Raceway
    - **Function:** External trunk routing cables, plumbing and flight termination system hardware along the hull.
    - **Position:** about 0.05 to 0.74 (estimate).
    - **Detail:** Redesigned on V3 for protection and serviceability. The high-pressure lines of the nosecone COPVs are visible on it.
    - **Sources:** NSF 2026-03-03, 2026-05-15.

16. **id: `rcs-midbody`**
    - **Name:** Mid-body thrusters
    - **Function:** Additional attitude control, especially for docking.
    - **Position:** about 0.45 to 0.50 (estimate; NSF says "about midway down the ship").
    - **Sources:** NSF 2026-05-15.

17. **id: `common-dome`**
    - **Name:** Common dome
    - **Function:** Single bulkhead separating methane (above) from LOX (below).
    - **Position:** weld about 0.42, crown about 0.38 (estimate).
    - **Detail:** It bulges downward so methane drains to the central transfer tube. V3 lowered it about 1.1 m. Flight termination system charges are also placed here on V3.
    - **Sources:** Ringwatchers s33-tanks; NSF 2026-05-15.

18. **id: `header-feedlines`**
    - **Name:** Header transfer lines
    - **Function:** Carry header-tank propellant from the nose to the engines for landing.
    - **Position:** about 0.06 to 0.95 (estimate).
    - **Detail:** Two long lines run down through the payload bay and both main tanks. V3 has 100% vacuum jacketing on the header feed system. On Flight 10 the ablative backup layer protected these lines where tiles had been removed.
    - **Sources:** SpaceX 2026-05-12; Ringwatchers s37-tps.

19. **id: `fuel-downcomers`**
    - **Name:** Fuel downcomers (transfer tubes)
    - **Function:** Carry methane from the main tank through the LOX tank to the engines.
    - **Position:** about 0.06 to 0.40 (estimate).
    - **Detail:** Four downcomers: three smaller ones feed the RVacs and a central one feeds the three sea-level engines. The central one connects to a sump (V2 layout; V3 propellant system was a "clean-sheet redesign" and its routing is not published).
    - **Sources:** Wikipedia pointer to NSF (2024-12-10); SpaceX 2026-05-12.

20. **id: `lox-main-tank`**
    - **Name:** LOX main tank
    - **Function:** Holds most of the liquid oxygen.
    - **Position:** about 0.12 (aft dome) to 0.42 (common dome) (estimate).
    - **Detail:** It is the larger of the two tanks, with about 48 columns of internal stringers (V2) and slosh baffles in its lower half. On V3 the RVac tops are recessed slightly into its bottom.
    - **Specs:** About 1,250 t LOX (estimate).
    - **Sources:** Ringwatchers s33-tanks; NSF 2026-05-15.

21. **id: `aft-flaps`**
    - **Name:** Aft flaps (2)
    - **Function:** Main aerodynamic control and drag surfaces at the tail during descent.
    - **Position:** about 0.02 to 0.23 (estimate); mounted across the aft section and the lower LOX barrel.
    - **Detail:** Same size on V3 as on V2, but the tiles are now pinned wherever possible. Each is driven by one actuator with three motors (V3), down from two actuators per flap. Flight 12 intentionally stress-tested them.
    - **Specs:** Dimensions not public.
    - **Sources:** SpaceX 2026-05-12, Flight 12 page; NSF 2026-05-15, 2026-03-13.

22. **id: `rcs-aft-flap-hinge`**
    - **Name:** Aft flap hinge thrusters
    - **Function:** Attitude control using gas routed through pipes in the aft flap hinges to a redirecting plate.
    - **Position:** about 0.10 to 0.20 (estimate).
    - **Sources:** NSF 2026-05-15.

23. **id: `ship-qd-plate`**
    - **Name:** Ship quick-disconnect (QD) plate
    - **Function:** Ground fill connection for LOX and methane, updated to double as the in-space propellant transfer port.
    - **Position:** about 0.05 to 0.10, leeward (estimate).
    - **Detail:** On V3 it is split into separate LOX and methane plates on the ship side (still one plate on the tower side) and sits between the two aft docking drogues. In September 2026 the tower's QD arm was reconnected to a hanging Ship 42 as a post-catch safing rehearsal.
    - **Sources:** NSF 2026-02-27, 2026-05-15, 2026-09-14; SpaceX 2025-10-30.

24. **id: `docking-drogues-aft`**
    - **Name:** Aft docking drogues (2)
    - **Function:** Second pair of passive docking fittings.
    - **Position:** about 0.06 to 0.11, leeward (estimate).
    - **Sources:** NSF 2026-05-15; SpaceX 2026-05-12.

25. **id: `aft-dome-thrust-puck`**
    - **Name:** Aft dome and thrust puck
    - **Function:** Bottom bulkhead of the LOX tank and the structure that takes engine thrust.
    - **Position:** dome weld about 0.11 to 0.13, puck about 0.06 (estimate).
    - **Detail:** The sea-level engines mount to the thrust puck at the bottom of the aft dome, and the RVacs mount to the dome around it. V3 lowered the aft dome about 1.1 m and flattened the puck.
    - **Sources:** NSF 2026-05-15; Wikipedia pointer to NSF video (2023-04-26).

26. **id: `attic`**
    - **Name:** Attic
    - **Function:** Small unpressurized space between the LOX tank bottom and the aft heat shield.
    - **Position:** about 0.085 to 0.12 (estimate).
    - **Detail:** On V2 it was a large volume where leaks caused fires (Flight 7). V3 deleted the large aft close-out volume and engine shrouds. The remaining attic covers only the RVac tops under a metal roof, with new vents.
    - **Sources:** SpaceX 2025-02-24 and 2026-05-12; NSF 2026-05-15.

27. **id: `rvac-engines`**
    - **Name:** Raptor 3 Vacuum engines (3)
    - **Function:** High-efficiency engines for the ascent burn in near-vacuum.
    - **Position:** 0.00 to 0.085.
    - **Detail:** Fixed (do not gimbal). One shut down early on both Flight 12 and Flight 14 without losing the mission.
    - **Specs:** 275 tf each; 2.3 m diameter, 4.4 m tall.
    - **Sources:** SpaceX vehicle page, 2026-05-12, Flight 12 and 14 pages; Ringwatchers s33-aft.

28. **id: `sl-raptor-engines`**
    - **Name:** Raptor 3 sea-level engines (3)
    - **Function:** Ascent thrust and steering, plus all in-space burns and landing so far.
    - **Position:** 0.00 to 0.056.
    - **Detail:** They gimbal for thrust vector control. A single engine performed the orbit insertion and deorbit burns on Flight 14; all three relit for the landing flip.
    - **Specs:** 250 tf each at sea level (goal 280 tf); 1,525 kg; 1.3 m diameter, 2.9 m tall.
    - **Sources:** SpaceX 2026-05-12, vehicle page, Flight 14 page.

29. **id: `engine-skirt`**
    - **Name:** Engine skirt and staging interface
    - **Function:** Unpressurized aft structure that carries the vehicle's weight on the pad and interfaces with the booster.
    - **Position:** 0.00 to about 0.12 to 0.14.
    - **Detail:** Clamps hold it to the booster. A shielded false ceiling protects hardware during hot staging (V2). On V2 it was just over 7 m tall with 156 internal stringer columns. Skirt vents dump gas and may act as thrusters.
    - **Sources:** Ringwatchers s33-aft.

30. **id: `heat-shield`**
    - **Name:** Heat shield (whole windward side)
    - **Function:** Protects the steel structure during reentry.
    - **Position:** 0.00 to 1.00 on the windward side; also wraps partly onto the leeward nose.
    - **Detail:** Hexagonal silica-based tiles on welded pins, over white felt insulation, over a black ablative backup layer, with felt gap filler between tiles. Special small tiles cover the stringer bands at dome welds. V3 adds a new pin design and pins almost everywhere. About 18,000 tiles on V1 (current count not public).
    - **Sources:** Ringwatchers s33-tps, s37-tps; NSF 2026-02-27, 2026-05-15, 2026-09-27; CNBC 2021.

31. **id: `avionics-power`**
    - **Name:** Avionics and power units
    - **Function:** Flight computers, batteries, inverters and high-voltage distribution.
    - **Position:** Distributed; not public.
    - **Detail:** About 60 units across ship and booster, about 9 MW peak, multi-sensor navigation, and RF propellant gauges for microgravity. More than 500 physical sensors.
    - **Sources:** SpaceX 2026-05-12, 2026-09-15.

### Variants (not part of the cutaway)

- **id: `variant-starlink`** Starlink ship: PEZ door and dispenser; operational since Flight 14 (26 Starlink V3). Official.
- **id: `variant-tanker`** Tanker: a V3 ship with docking probes added, carrying propellant to another ship. Official concept; not yet flown.
- **id: `variant-depot`** Propellant depot: long-duration storage ship in orbit. SpaceX completed a "depot power module demonstration" HLS milestone. Details not public.
- **id: `variant-hls`** Human Landing System:
  - No flaps or heat shield for Earth return.
  - Landing legs (a full-scale leg was drop-tested onto simulated regolith), a crew elevator and two airlocks of about 13 m3 each.
  - More than 600 m3 pressurized volume.
  - Docks with Orion using an androgynous system based on Dragon 2's.
  - Body-mounted solar arrays and mid-body landing thrusters appear in renders (reported, not officially specified).
  - Sources: SpaceX 2025-10-30; Wikipedia pointer to Space Explored, 2023-11-02.
- **id: `variant-cargo-lander`** Cargo lander: up to 100 t to the lunar surface. Official.
- **id: `variant-crew`** Crew ship: "up to 100 people on long-duration, interplanetary flights" (SpaceX vehicle page). SpaceX has announced a planned crewed circumlunar flight within 200 km of the Moon (SpaceX update, 2026-05-21).
- **id: `variant-v4`** Future V4 ship:
  - Nine engines (three sea-level, six RVac) per Musk's May 2025 presentation (NSF 2025-05-30).
  - About 200 t payload "potentially as soon as Starship V4" (S-1).
  - The FAA analyzed an envelope of up to 9 ship engines and up to 2,650 t of ship propellant (FAA Final EIS, Jan 2026).

## Uncertain or conflicting

- **Expendable payload for V3:** not published by SpaceX. Musk's 2024 posts ("V3 ... ~200 tons with full reusability and ~400 tons expendable", 2024-03-15; "close to 200 tons ... as a tanker", 2024-07) used the old numbering, in which "V3" meant a roughly 150 m stack (Ars Technica, 2024-04-08). That design is now called V4 or "Future Starship" (142 m, shown May 2025). The flown V3 is 124 m and rated 100+ t reusable. Treat any "V3 = 200 t" claim as referring to V4.
- **Dry mass:** no official V2 or V3 number. Figures in circulation include "roughly 100 t" (Musk about V1, 2021), 85 t for V2 (Wikipedia cites a weak secondary source), and NSF's passing "160-metric-ton vehicle" (2026-09-27), which may include residuals or be a loose figure. Present as unknown.
- **V2 payload of about 35 t:** press figure, not an official SpaceX number.
- **LOX and methane split:** not published. The 1,250 t / 350 t split is an estimate from an assumed O/F ratio of about 3.6. Ringwatchers' V2 tank volumes (about 1,030 m3 LOX, 790 m3 CH4) are model-based and imply slightly more than 1,500 t at subcooled densities, so they are not precise.
- **V3 dome move:** NSF reports both the common dome and the aft dome moved down about 1.106 m. It is not clear whether both moved by that same amount, and the V3 aft skirt height is not public.
- **Header tanks:** V3 header capacities are not public. That the methane header is in the nosecone is confirmed for the orbital-class ships (Ship 24 to 32) and V2 (Ringwatchers), and NSF (2026-03-13) says the V3 nosecone "houses the two header tanks". Some popular explanations still place the methane header inside the LOX tank, which described the 2020 to 2021 suborbital prototypes; that older layout was not verified against a primary source in this pass.
- **Starlink antenna locations on V3:** NSF 2026-03-03 says the array moved "from its previous location below the payload bay to the nose cone"; NSF 2026-05-15 says two dishes are on the sides of the heat shield and two on the leeward side. Exact positions are uncertain.
- **Reaction control type on V3:** SpaceX says "more efficient reaction control systems" but does not say whether V3 uses cold-gas ullage thrusters (the historical approach) or hot-gas methane-oxygen thrusters. Thruster count and thrust are not public.
- **Flap dimensions, flap motor supplier, battery chemistry and capacity:** not public. The Tesla-motor detail dates from 2021 prototypes.
- **Heat-shield figures:** the tile count (about 18,000) is a 2021 figure, and the V3 count is not public. Tile material is reported as silica-based, but SpaceX has not published the composition. Peak reentry heating, angle of attack and heat flux for V3 are not public. The 1,650 K figure comes from a 2019 test.
- **Metallic tiles:** tested on Flight 10. Gerstenmaier reportedly said they "didn't work so well" (September 2025 AAS talk, via press). This dossier could not retrieve the primary Ars Technica article, so treat the quote as reported.
- **Catch hardware:** V3 catch-point geometry and exact location are not public (NSF only says "higher up" on the nose than V2's). As of 2026-09-29 no ship catch has been attempted; Flight 15 is the next candidate, pending FAA approval.
- **Propellant transfer:** no ship-to-ship transfer has been attempted as of 2026-09-29. Timing is not announced, and the tanker count needed for HLS has been quoted anywhere from "four to eight" to "high teens" in past years (not verified in this pass).
- **Raptor 3 Isp and thrust in vacuum for the sea-level engine:** not published on SpaceX's V3 materials. The 263 tf figure is derived from SpaceX's 1,614 tf ship total.
- **Height:** SpaceX rounds to 52 m. NSF says V3 external dimensions are unchanged from V2 (52.1 m by common reporting).
- **Section positions in Components:** these are estimates from ring counts and could be off by 1 to 2 m in places.
